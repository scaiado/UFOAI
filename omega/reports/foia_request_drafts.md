## How to use these

These are drafts for **you** to review, edit, and file yourself. They have not been sent and
cannot be sent from here. Before you file any of them:

1. **Fill in the requester block.** Every letter has `[Requester Name]`, `[Street Address]`,
   `[City, State ZIP]`, `[Email]`, `[Phone]`, and `[Date]` placeholders. Agencies reject or
   stall requests with incomplete contact information.

2. **Verify the office and address.** Each letter names the agency's standard FOIA office by
   its publicly known designation, but every letter carries a bracketed note to verify the
   current mailing address and office symbol at the agency's own FOIA page before sending.
   FOIA office symbols and addresses change; an invented street address is worse than no
   street address. **Do not send a paper letter to an address you have not verified.**

3. **Prefer the portal.** Most agencies now accept submissions electronically. **FOIA.gov**
   routes to most executive-branch agencies and is the right default. Several agencies on this
   list run their own portals instead of, or in addition to, FOIA.gov — notably CIA, NSA, FBI
   (`efoia.fbi.gov`), the Department of Defense components (which use a shared DoD FOIA
   request portal), and NARA. Check FOIA.gov first; it will redirect you where an
   agency-specific portal is required. Paste the letter body into the portal's narrative field
   rather than retyping it, so the full scope and the fee-waiver and segregability language
   are captured in the record.

4. **Review the fee-waiver claim before sending.** Every letter requests a waiver under
   5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(A)(iii) with the two-part justification (public interest / not
   primarily commercial). The claim is only as good as its factual predicate. If you do not
   actually have a channel for disseminating what you obtain — a publication, a site, a
   preprint, a dataset release, an institutional affiliation — the waiver will be denied and
   you will have spent a month on the fee fight instead of the search. Edit the bracketed
   dissemination sentence in each letter to describe your real channel, specifically. Also
   decide your fee cap; each letter has a `[$ amount]` placeholder for the maximum you will
   pay if the waiver is denied in whole or part.

5. **Review the expedited-processing claim before sending.** Expedited processing under
   5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(6)(E) is requested in **Letter 12 only** (AARO/DoD), under the
   "compelling need" prong at § 552(a)(6)(E)(v)(II) — urgency to inform the public about
   actual or alleged federal government activity, for a requester primarily engaged in
   disseminating information. That prong has a factual predicate too: you must be a person
   primarily engaged in disseminating information. If you are not, **delete that section from
   Letter 12.** The other twelve letters omit expedited processing deliberately. Requests for
   1947–1969 historical records have no genuine urgency basis, and asserting one invites a
   denial that adds a round of appeal to your timeline for nothing. A § 552(a)(6)(E)(v)(II)
   argument is also arguably available for Letters 8 and 9 (current military aviator reporting
   and flight-safety relevance) if your dissemination predicate is strong; it is left out of
   those drafts as the weaker call, and you can add it from Letter 12's text if you decide
   otherwise. The (v)(I) prong — imminent threat to life or physical safety — does not
   plausibly apply to anything on this list; do not assert it.

6. **A note on scope discipline.** Each letter is deliberately narrow and date-bounded. Broad
   "all records about UFOs" requests are the single most common cause of a FOIA denial for
   failure to reasonably describe the records sought. If an agency tells you a request is
   still too broad, every letter includes an offer to narrow rather than be denied — use it,
   in writing, and keep the correspondence.

7. **What is not here.** Letter 13 is addressed to DoD, not to any contractor. FOIA reaches
   federal agencies, not private corporations — see the note at the end of that letter.

## Appeal mechanics (read before you file, not after)

**The 20-working-day clock.** The agency must make a determination within 20 working days of
receipt, 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(6)(A)(i), extendable by 10 additional working days in "unusual
circumstances" under § 552(a)(6)(B)(i), with written notice. A "determination" means telling
you the scope of what it will release and withhold and your appeal rights — not merely
acknowledging receipt and assigning a tracking number.

**Administrative appeal deadline.** When an agency issues an adverse determination — a denial
in full or part, a Glomar response, a no-records response you believe reflects an inadequate
search, a fee-category or fee-waiver denial, or a denial of expedited processing — it must
notify you of your right to appeal. Since the FOIA Improvement Act of 2016, agencies must
allow you **not less than 90 days** from the date of the adverse determination to file an
administrative appeal. Some agency regulations and older determination letters still recite
shorter windows (30 or 60 days); the 90-day floor controls, but **do not test it** — file
within whatever shorter window the letter states if you can, and calendar the deadline the
day the determination arrives. Appeals go to the agency's designated appellate authority named
in the determination letter, not back to the processing office.

**Exhaustion is a prerequisite to suit.** Judicial review lies in federal district court under
5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B), but you must ordinarily exhaust administrative remedies first — that
is, actually file the administrative appeal and let it be decided — before you can sue. See
*Oglesby v. U.S. Department of the Army*, 920 F.2d 57 (D.C. Cir. 1990). Two practical
consequences:

- **Constructive exhaustion.** If the agency fails to comply with the statutory time limits,
  you are deemed to have exhausted administrative remedies under § 552(a)(6)(C)(i) and may
  file suit. But if the agency issues its determination before you file, the right to sue on
  constructive exhaustion evaporates and you must appeal administratively after all. In
  practice, constructive exhaustion is leverage for a phone call with the FOIA Public Liaison
  far more often than it is a reason to litigate.
- **Issue preservation.** Raise *every* ground in the administrative appeal — adequacy of
  search, each exemption, segregability, the fee waiver, the fee category. Grounds you omit
  may be treated as unexhausted.

**Two no-cost intermediate steps, both worth using before you think about litigation.**
First, the agency's **FOIA Public Liaison**, whose job is to take requester complaints about
service and help resolve them; every determination and acknowledgment letter should name one.
Second, the **Office of Government Information Services (OGIS)** at the National Archives,
the statutory FOIA ombudsman under 5 U.S.C. § 552(h), which offers free mediation between
requesters and agencies. Using OGIS does not toll your appeal deadline — file the appeal,
then mediate.

**Fee-waiver denials.** A fee-waiver denial is itself an adverse determination you must appeal
administratively to preserve. On judicial review, a court reviews the fee-waiver denial
**de novo** but is limited to the administrative record before the agency, § 552(a)(4)(A)(vii)
— which is the reason to put your full public-interest showing in the request letter itself
rather than saving it for later.

**Record-keeping.** Keep the submitted text, the submission confirmation, the tracking number,
every agency letter with its date, and your appeal with proof of filing date. If this ever
becomes a complaint, that file is the case.

### Note on why this letter goes to DoD and not to a contractor

**FOIA does not reach private corporations.** The statute's disclosure obligations run to
federal "agencies" — executive departments, military departments, government corporations,
government-controlled corporations, and other establishments and independent regulatory
agencies in the executive branch, as defined in 5 U.S.C. § 552(f)(1). A defense contractor,
however large and however much of its revenue comes from federal contracts, is not an agency,
and a FOIA request addressed to one has no legal effect. Sending one accomplishes nothing and
may compromise the credibility of a research project.

**What does reach contractor information is this letter.** Records that a contractor submits to
the government, and records the government creates about a contractor, are **agency records**
in the Department's possession and are subject to FOIA — subject to the exemptions, principally
Exemption 4 for genuinely confidential commercial information, which is why Section 5 of this
letter addresses that exemption at length. The practical route to contractor-related material
is therefore always through the contracting agency, never the contractor.

**Two adjacent routes, for completeness.** First, federal procurement data — contract numbers,
awardees, values, periods of performance, and competition status — is published outside FOIA
through federal spending transparency systems, and should be mined there first, because it is
free, immediate, and the best source of the specific contract numbers that make a follow-up
FOIA request fast. Second, if a contractor is a federally funded research and development center
or operates a government-owned facility, some of its records may be agency records held by the
sponsoring agency; that is still a request to the agency, not to the operator.
